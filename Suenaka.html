The Tradition

About

Suenaka-kei Tetsugaku-hō Aikidō continues the study and practice of the aikidō transmitted by Roy Yukio Suenaka.

Rooted in Charleston, South Carolina, the tradition emphasizes practical martial effectiveness, disciplined training, and the philosophical principles of aikidō.

Its governing association, the Suenaka-kei Yūdanshakai, is being formed to bring together qualified practitioners and support continued transmission of the art.

The historical material on this page summarizes the biographical record published by Suenaka.com. It describes Roy Suenaka's own teachers, ranks, and work; it does not imply that the new association has inherited or issued those credentials.

The Lineage Founder

Roy Yukio Suenaka

末中 幸男
末中 幸男
1940–2020
Born in Honolulu, Hawaii
Charleston, South Carolina

Portrait image linked from Suenaka.com.

Roy Yukio Suenaka was born in Honolulu, Hawaii. His father, Warren Kenji Suenaka, introduced him to budō and selected instructors for his early martial education. The published biography names teachers from several disciplines, including Henry Seishiro Okazaki, James Masayoshi Mitose, Yukiso Yamamoto, and kendō instructor Shūji Mikami.

Suenaka began aikidō study during Kōichi Tōhei's 1953 visit to Hawaii. According to the published biography, he later studied at Aikikai Hombu under Morihei Ueshiba beginning in 1961, and received a menkyo kaiden certificate from Ueshiba that year. The source describes his direct study there as lasting eight years.

His training also included extended study of Matsumura Seito and Hakutsuru Shorin-ryū karate and kobudō with Hohan Sōken, and jūdō and jūjutsu study at the Kōdōkan with Kazuo Itō. The biography also records training in kendō, boxing, lua, kung fu, and several karate traditions.

Suenaka moved to Charleston in 1972 and served as the Southeastern U.S. director for Kōichi Tōhei's International Ki Society until 1975. Suenaka.com records that he then resigned and formed the American International Ki Development and Philosophical Society (AIKDPS). His tradition was named Suenaka-ha Tetsugaku-hō Wadōkai Aikidō.

He authored Complete Aikido: Aikido Kyohan, published in 1998.

Historical naming: Suenaka-ha Tetsugaku-hō Wadōkai Aikidō is Roy Suenaka's historically named tradition. Suenaka-kei Tetsugaku-hō Aikidō is the name adopted for this present lineage initiative. The names are related, but they refer to distinct institutional stages.

Historical Record

Ranks & Awards

The following qualifications and awards are reported in Suenaka.com's published biography.

They belong to Roy Suenaka's personal history. They are not credentials awarded by the Suenaka-kei Yūdanshakai, which is currently in formation.

  • Menkyo kaiden in aikidō (1961): a teaching certificate awarded by Morihei Ueshiba, as reported in the biography.
  • Hachidan in aikidō (1977): eighth-degree black belt, as reported by Suenaka.com.
  • Hachidan in Matsumura Seito Hakutsuru Shorin-ryū karate and kobudō (1977): as reported by the biography.
  • Rokudan in Matsumura Seito Hakutsuru Shorin-ryū karate and kobudō (1972): awarded by Hohan Sōken, according to the source.
  • Sandan in Kōdōkan jūdō and jūjutsu (1970): dual certificates attributed to Kazuo Itō and Risei Kanō in the published biography.
  • Nidan in kendō (1958): awarded by Shūji Mikami, according to the source.
  • Hawaiian State Golden Gloves boxing: participation during 1954–1958, as listed in the biography.

The source lists an eighth dan in aikidō but does not identify the awarding organization in that entry. The site therefore records the rank as a biographical claim rather than assigning it to Aikikai or another organization.

Instruction & Service

Teaching Experience

Suenaka.com's biography describes instruction spanning dōjō, schools, military programs, police organizations, seminars, and specialist training.

The entries below summarize that source's account; they are not independently verified by this page.

  • Assisted with aikidō instruction at Hawaii Aikikai after receiving his first aikidō black belt in 1956; the biography places this work through his 1958 entry into the U.S. Air Force.
  • Opened an aikidō school in Sacramento, California, in 1958, which the biography describes as the first recognized aikidō school in the continental United States; taught there until his 1961 assignment to Japan.
  • Taught the California Highway Patrol and Military Police at Mather Air Force Base from 1958 to 1961.
  • Taught jūdō and aikidō at Encino High School in 1960.
  • Taught young people and counselors at Boys Town in Placerville, California, between 1958 and 1960.
  • Served as a guest instructor in the U.S. Air Force Strategic Air Command Survival Training Course at Stead Air Force Base, Nevada, from 1958 to 1961.
  • During assignments in Japan, Okinawa, and Vietnam from 1961 to 1972, taught hand-to-hand combat to military and police groups, including Military Police, Marines, Army Green Berets, Navy SEALs, the Air Commando program, and the Ryukyu Police Department in Naha.
  • Opened an aikidō dōjō in Okinawa in 1961. The biography describes challenges from local karate practitioners as part of that school's early experience.
  • Opened an aikidō and karate dōjō in Charleston in 1973. The biography described it as still operating when that page was published.
  • Taught Air Force Tactical Recovery Teams in Charleston from 1973 to 1974 and instructed Air Force Security Police in a special program initiated by General Sadler.
  • Instructed at the South Carolina Police Academy in Columbia in 1988–1989, including State Police, FBI, city and county SWAT teams, SLED, and correctional officers.
  • Taught Costa Rican special task forces and Presidential Secret Service personnel in 1991 in a program sponsored by the Charleston City Police Department.
  • Taught a 1994 course for female and smaller-framed officers serving in the Charleston City Police Department.
  • The biography also lists instruction for law-enforcement employees, jailers, bodyguards, State Department officials, Border Patrol, SWAT, bounty hunters, bail bondsmen, DEA, U.S. Marshals, and Foreign Dignitary Protection Service agents.
  • Conducted annual seminars in South Carolina, Georgia, Florida, North Carolina, Virginia, and New York, with special seminars in California, Washington, and Colorado.
  • Authored Complete Aikido: Aikido Kyohan, published by Tuttle in Boston in 1998, according to the source.

The Martial Art

Aikidō

合気道

The Aikidō of Roy Suenaka

Suenaka's aikidō drew on instruction from Morihei Ueshiba and Kōichi Tōhei, alongside a wide-ranging martial education. His published biography identifies his independent tradition as Suenaka-ha Tetsugaku-hō Wadōkai Aikidō.

Training emphasized effective self-defense, balance, coordinated body movement, blending with attacks, throws, joint controls, and development of mind and body.

Equal attention was given to physical ability and personal development.

The practice encouraged self-discipline, moderation, compassion, and respect for others.

Suenaka-kei studies this inheritance while developing its own documented curriculum and governance.

Recognition & Certification

Rank & Transmission

段位と伝承

Suenaka-kei Tetsugaku-hō Aikidō distinguishes recognized aikidō rank from proficiency and transmission within its own developing tradition.

The intended system provides two complementary forms of recognition: Aikikai rank and Suenaka-kei certificates and licenses administered through its governing Yūdanshakai.

External Rank Recognition

Aikikai Kyū / Dan

合気会・級位／段位

The intended rank pathway follows the Aikikai system of kyū and dan grades.

Dan grades are examined, recognized, and registered with Aikikai Hombu through appropriately authorized Aikikai examination and registration channels.

Advancement remains subject to Aikikai requirements, qualifying practice, examination, and approval by authorized examiners.

Kyū (級) Student grades preceding black belt rank. Dan (段) Recognized black belt and higher grades.

The Yūdanshakai does not independently award Aikikai dan grades.

Internal Proficiency & Transmission

Suenaka-kei

末中系・目録／免許

Proficiency in Suenaka-kei Tetsugaku-hō Aikidō is to be evaluated and documented independently by the Suenaka-kei Yūdanshakai.

The proposed certification system distinguishes demonstrated technical knowledge from the authority entrusted to teach.

Mokuroku (目録) Certificate or register of transmission.

Documents a defined body of techniques, principles, and proficiency received and demonstrated by the practitioner.

Menkyo (免許) License of teaching authorization.

Grants permission to teach the particular body of material specified in the license.

Levels of Transmission

Shoden (初伝) Initial Transmission Mokuroku / Menkyo
Chūden (中伝) Intermediate Transmission Mokuroku / Menkyo
Okuden (奥伝) Deep Transmission Mokuroku / Menkyo

Aikikai rank and Suenaka-kei transmission credentials are separate qualifications. Neither automatically grants the other. A teaching license does not, by itself, authorize its holder to issue further certificates or licenses. This framework is under development and does not represent historical licenses issued by Roy Suenaka.

Governance

The Yūdanshakai

末中系有段者会 Suenaka-kei Yūdanshakai Suenaka-Lineage Dan-Ranked Practitioners Association

The Suenaka-kei Yūdanshakai is being established as the governing association of Suenaka-kei Tetsugaku-hō Aikidō.

The association is intended to unite qualified dan-ranked practitioners connected through Roy Suenaka's teachings.

Its proposed responsibilities include maintaining the technical curriculum, evaluating Suenaka-kei proficiency, administering certificates of transmission and teaching licenses, and preserving the continuity of the tradition.

Aikikai rank recognition and registration will remain under the authority of appropriately authorized Aikikai organizations.

Founding membership, examination standards, and formal governing authority are currently being established.

Continuity Across Generations

Historical Precedent

伝承と継続

A martial tradition must be capable of continuing beyond the lifetime of the teacher who holds it together.

Roy Suenaka's own independent founding provides an important precedent for the work now undertaken by Suenaka-kei.

Approximately six years separate Morihei Ueshiba's death from Suenaka's independent founding. A comparable interval separates Suenaka's death from the beginning of this present initiative.

First Generation

From Ō-Sensei to Suenaka

1969–1975

  1. 1969 Morihei Ueshiba dies. The founder of aikidō passes away on April 26.
  2. 1974 Kōichi Tōhei leaves Aikikai. A major division follows differences within aikidō leadership.
  3. 1975 Roy Suenaka establishes an independent organization. He resigns from the International Ki Society and forms AIKDPS, according to his published biography.
Present Generation

From Suenaka to Suenaka-kei

2020–2026

  1. 2020 Roy Yukio Suenaka dies. Suenaka passes away on February 8.
  2. 2020–2026 The transmission continues. Suenaka's students and their successors continue studying and teaching in different settings.
  3. 2026 Suenaka-kei Tetsugaku-hō Aikidō is named. Formation of the Suenaka-kei Yūdanshakai begins as a proposed framework for preserving and transmitting the tradition.

Fidelity to the source must outlast allegiance to an intermediary.

Roy Suenaka's example demonstrates that institutional independence and continuing fidelity to one's teachers need not be opposites. Preserving a martial tradition can require the establishment of new institutions.

Suenaka-kei seeks to carry this responsibility forward through documented proficiency, accountable teaching authority, and continuity of knowledge beyond changes in leadership or organizational membership.

This is a historical parallel, not a claim that the circumstances or causes of the two organizational transitions were identical. The first sequence is historical; the second records the founding direction of an association in formation.
